# MORTIS
### Book Zero of the Earth Reborn Epic

**By Raymond Bayly**

## Chapter One: The Speaker and the Shadow

The autumn wind howling off the northern crags carried the faint, iron tang of slaughter.

Lord Speaker Darius Spencer stood before the tall arched windows of the High Keep, staring out over the sprawling city of Oakhaven. Below, the slate-roofed tenements and twisting cobbled lanes of the capital were already sinking into dusk, shrouded in the gray gloom of gathering fog. Torches flickered along the city walls like dying embers in a hearth.

Behind him, soft footsteps clicked across the marble floor with deliberate, measured grace.

“It is not like him to make someone of your stature wait, Lord Speaker,” the woman said, her voice smooth as polished stone and cold as river ice.

Darius did not turn immediately. He kept his hands resting lightly on the hilt of his ceremonial broadsword, his knuckles rough and scarred from decades in the saddle. At fifty-two, Darius carried the heavy, broad-shouldered frame of a veteran campaigner who had spent more of his youth sleeping in mud and blood than in feather beds. His dark hair was frosted with gray at the temples, but his steel-gray eyes remained sharp enough to cut glass.

“The King has buried three wives, Arianna,” Darius replied quietly, watching her reflection resolve in the darkening windowpane. “Now he must bury his only son. A man in such grief does not keep appointments.”

The woman stepped into the circle of firelight cast by the massive stone hearth. Lady Arianna was a fixture of the royal court whom few truly understood and fewer dared cross. Her face possessed the austere, severe beauty of an ancient marble statue—high cheekbones, pale porcelain skin untouched by the sun, and dark eyes that seemed to absorb light rather than reflect it. She wore high-collared robes of midnight-blue velvet trimmed with silver fur, her long obsidian hair pinned with hairpins carved from black bone.

Thirty years ago, she had arrived in Oakhaven as a scholar from the eastern reaches, carrying ancient treatises on natural philosophy and the forgotten histories of the First Age. Over the decades, monarchs and ministers had come and gone, yet Arianna remained: indispensable, watchful, and terrifyingly serene.

“Grief is a luxury for common farmers, Darius,” Arianna said, coming to stand beside him. The faint scent of lavender, old parchment, and something dry and bitter—like crushed hemlock—wafted from her robes. “When a Crown Prince is butchered within his own bedchambers behind locked oak doors, grief must give way to iron. The border lords are already mobilizing their garrisons. Inquisitor Malakor has doubled the temple watch. If King Aldous does not act tonight, there will be no kingdom left to rule by morning.”

“Malakor is a zealot who smells heresy in every draft of wind,” Darius countered, finally turning to face her. “He wants to blame the northern clans. He wants holy war, because holy war fills the church’s coffers and justifies the pyres.”

“And what do you smell, Lord Speaker?” Arianna’s dark eyes locked onto his, unblinking.

Darius looked away toward the heavy double doors of the King’s private solar. Two guards in the scarlet cloaks of the Royal Guard stood stiffly at attention, their halberds trembling ever so slightly.

“I smell treason,” Darius murmured. “Valen was twenty-two years old, trained by the finest sword-masters in the realm. He didn't die to a northern raider who slipped over thirty-foot battlements unnoticed. Whoever killed the prince knew the guard rotations. They knew the latch-codes to the royal wing. And they left something behind that terrified even the Captain of the Guard.”

Arianna’s thin lips curved into the faintest hint of a smile. “Then you have not yet seen the body.”

“The King ordered the chamber sealed until I arrived,” Darius said, narrowing his eyes. “Why? Have you seen him?”

Before Arianna could answer, the heavy oak doors of the royal solar creaked open. A young page, his face pale as fresh parchment and eyes wide with terror, bowed shakily in the threshold.

“The King will receive you now, my lords,” the boy stammered, barely able to keep his voice from cracking. “Please... enter with haste.”

## Chapter Two: The Cold Slab

The royal crypts beneath the Great Keep smelled of ancient lime, damp stone, and the sickly-sweet copper stench of blood that refused to congeal.

Darius followed the flickering torchlight down the spiraling stone stairs, his boots echoing hollowly against the bedrock. At the foot of the descent, past the sarcophagi of kings who had ruled during the golden centuries before the Fall, a heavy iron-banded door stood ajar.

Inside the royal mortuary, the air was freezing. Great blocks of lake ice, hauled down from the northern peaks and packed in sawdust, lined the perimeter of the chamber to preserve the dead. In the center of the room, beneath the harsh white glow of oil-fed surgical lanterns, rested a heavy slab of gray granite.

A man in a blood-smeared leather apron stood beside the table, carefully arranging an array of silver scalpels, bone saws, and glass specimen jars.

“Murat,” Darius called out.

The mortician looked up, pushing brass-rimmed spectacles up the bridge of his crooked nose with the back of a leather-gloved forearm. Murat was forty-five, gaunt, with thinning black hair and yellowed teeth worn down from grinding them in his sleep. He had served as royal coroner for two decades, examining suicides, poisoned nobles, and battlefield casualties. He had seen every manner of violence mortal men could inflict upon one another.

Yet as Darius stepped into the light, he saw that Murat’s hands were shaking.

“Lord Speaker,” Murat said, his voice a raspy whisper that sounded like dry leaves scraping over pavement. “I was told you were coming. I wish to God you weren't.”

“Show me,” Darius said, stopping at the edge of the slab.

Murat took a ragged breath and pulled back the heavy linen shroud.

Darius gripped the edge of the stone table, his stomach clenching violently. He had seen men torn in half by trebuchet fire; he had walked through plague pits outside Dever where thousands had died screaming. Nothing in his fifty-two years of life had prepared him for the ruin of Crown Prince Valen.

The young prince lay naked upon the cold granite, his handsome, noble features frozen in an expression of sheer, unadulterated horror. His mouth was locked open in a silent scream, his teeth shattered as if he had clamped his jaw shut with enough force to splinter bone.

But it was not mortal wounds that had ended his life. There were no stab wounds, no defensive slashes on his forearms, no ligature marks on his throat.

From the prince’s right fingertips to the base of his collarbone, the skin had blackened and crystallized into something that looked like charred obsidian. Faint, intricate patterns—geometric lines and microscopic circuits resembling silver spiderwebs—were etched directly into the necrotic flesh, glowing with a faint, dying phosphorescence that pulsed every few seconds like the fading heartbeat of a dying star.

“What in the name of the First Gods am I looking at?” Darius whispered, leaning closer.

“It isn’t poison, my lord,” Murat said, carefully picking up a pair of long silver tweezers. “And it isn't any blade forged by human hands. Look here.”

Murat pointed the tweezers at the prince’s right wrist. The radial artery was visible through the blackened skin, but the blood inside was not red or dark maroon. It was a thick, viscous black gel that had crystallized inside the veins, hardening like cooled volcanic glass.

“When I made the initial incision to draw fluid,” Murat continued, his voice trembling, “the tissue didn’t bleed. It chimed. Like struck crystal. And look at his chest.”

Murat used a wooden probe to part the sternum where an incision had already been made. Inside the thoracic cavity, the prince’s heart had withered into a shriveled knot of black carbon. Tiny metallic filaments, thinner than human hair, were woven through the pericardium, anchoring into the spine.

“This was not an assassination, Darius,” Murat said, looking up with haunted eyes. “This was an extraction. Someone—or something—fed on his life force, using his neural pathways as a conduit to draw out whatever was stored inside him.”

Darius felt a cold bead of sweat roll down the nape of his neck. “What was stored inside him?”

“Forbidden knowledge,” came a soft, melodic voice from the shadowed doorway.

Darius spun around, his hand instinctively going to his sword hilt.

Lady Arianna stepped into the mortuary chamber, her dark robes brushing the frost-rimmed floor. She looked upon the butchered prince not with horror or disgust, but with a terrifying, clinical fascination.

“The Prince was a collector of relics, Lord Speaker,” Arianna whispered, stepping to the edge of the slab and reaching out a pale hand toward the glowing runes on the dead boy's arm. “He believed he had found the key to the machines that slept before the world ended. And it seems the machines... found him first.”

## Chapter Three: Blood in the Silk

The Prince’s bedchambers in the eastern wing of the High Keep felt like the inside of a tomb.

Captain Vane of the Royal Guard stood in the doorway, his hand resting on his pommel, watching with hollow eyes as Darius and Arianna surveyed the scene. Four guardsmen in heavy mail stood at the corners of the room, their eyes fixed firmly on the floor, visibly unnerved by the unnatural chill that lingered in the air.

The chamber was an opulent testament to royal privilege: silk tapestries depicting ancient victories of the House of Aldous, heavy mahogany bookshelves overflowing with leather-bound tomes, and an enormous four-poster bed draped in crimson velvet.

The bed was a slaughterhouse.

The velvet hangings were shredded into ribbons, hanging in tatters like the webs of some colossal predatory spider. Deep gouges were torn into the solid oak posts—grooves three inches deep, as if carved by steel claws. Frost coated the bedsheets, and in the center of the mattress lay a pool of frozen black sludge that smelled of ozone and scorched copper.

“The doors were bolted from the inside,” Captain Vane said, his voice tight with defensive strain. “Two guardsmen were posted outside in the hallway all night. They swear on their mothers’ souls that no one entered or left. At three in the morning, they heard a sound like glass breaking, followed by a single gasp. When they breached the door with the master key, the room was empty—save for the Prince’s corpse on the floor.”

“And the window?” Darius asked, walking to the far wall where double glass doors opened onto a stone balcony overlooking the vertical drop of the cliff face.

“Bolted,” Vane replied. “The frost was on the inside of the glass. Nothing scaled that wall, Speaker. It’s two hundred feet of sheer granite above the river.”

Darius crouched by the bed, running his bare fingers over the gouged oak. The wood wasn't just scratched; it was splintered and chemically degraded, the fibers brittle as ash.

“Vane, where is the chambermaid who found him?” Darius asked without looking up.

Vane hesitated. “In the anteroom, sir. She’s hysterical. We’ve kept her under guard since dawn.”

“Bring her in.”

A moment later, two guards ushered in a young girl no older than eighteen. Her gray servant’s dress was torn at the shoulder, and her blonde hair was plastered to her sweat-streaked face. Her eyes were bloodshot and wild with panic, darting between the shredded bed and Lady Arianna’s imposing figure.

“Peace, child,” Darius said gently, standing up and holding out an open, empty hand. “You are Clara, yes? You attend the royal bedchambers?”

The girl swallowed hard and gave a jerky nod, wrapping her arms around her trembling ribs. “Yes, my lord. I... I brought the morning tea at five. The guards let me in with the master key because the Prince didn't answer his wake-chime.”

“Tell me what you saw, Clara,” Darius said, his voice low, steady, and soothing. “Not what the guards told you to say. What did you see when you crossed that threshold?”

Clara began to weep, quiet, racking sobs that shook her entire frame. “The room was so cold, my lord. My breath... it was like standing in a snowstorm. The candles had burned down to black stumps. I saw His Highness lying on the rug by the hearth. At first I thought he had fallen in his drink. But then...”

She trailed off, her eyes wide with terror, staring into the dark corners of the ceiling.

“Then what, Clara?” Arianna asked softly, stepping forward.

The girl flinched, shrinking away from Arianna as if from an open flame. “There was a shadow, my lady. Up near the rafters. It wasn't a man. It had wings, like a great bird, but it had arms like a spider. It was whispering to him. In words that sounded like metal scraping together.”

“Did it speak to you?” Darius pressed, stepping between Clara and Arianna.

“No, my lord,” Clara whispered, tears spilling down her cheeks. “It looked at me. It had two glowing eyes, red like coals in a furnace. And then it... it shattered. It didn’t fly out the window. It broke into a hundred black crows that flew straight into the stone wall and vanished into the mortar.”

Vane scoffed loudly from the doorway. “Superstitious nonsense! The girl is mad with grief. Shadows turning into birds? It’s peasant tavern tales!”

“Is it?” Arianna murmured, walking to the solid stone wall where the Prince’s writing desk sat.

She reached down, her pale fingers brushing the mortar between two massive granite blocks. She pulled her hand back, showing Darius her fingertips.

They were coated in fine black soot and a single, glossy crow feather made entirely of razor-sharp flexible steel.

“This was no phantom, Captain Vane,” Arianna said, holding the metallic feather up to the torchlight. “This was an operative of the Old World. And they have just declared war on the crown.”

## Chapter Four: Council of Wolves

The Grand Council chamber was a cauldron of shouting men, clanking armor, and righteous fury.

Fifty lords, guild-masters, and regional commanders sat along the tiered oak benches of the amphitheater, their voices echoing off the vaulted stone ceiling in a deafening cacophony. At the head of the chamber, beneath the golden banner of the crowned dragon, sat King Aldous IV upon the Sun Throne.

The King looked as if ten years had been stolen from him overnight. His silver crown sat askew upon greasy, unkempt white hair; his robe of state hung loosely on his trembling shoulders; and his rheumy eyes darted frantically across the shouting assembly like a trapped animal surrounded by hounds.

To his right stood High Inquisitor Malakor.

Malakor was a towering, austere figure draped in the blood-red and white vestments of the Church of Aurtus. His head was shaved bald, and across his forehead was branded the twin-flame sigil of the Inquisitorial Order. In his right hand he held his six-foot silver staff of office, topped with a barbed sunburst.

“Blood demands blood!” Malakor thundered, slamming the base of his silver staff into the marble dais with a crack that silenced the hall. “The Crown Prince was slaughtered by dark sorcery! The border clans of Dever have harbored witches and heretics who consort with the demon-machines of the First Age! While the Lord Speaker counsels patience and diplomacy, the blood of our royal house cries out from the stone for vengeance!”

A chorus of roared approval rose from the northern lords, several banging their fists and pommels against the wooden desks.

“The Inquisitor is right!” shouted Duke Torval, a burly, bearded warlord from the iron-mining valleys. “Mobilize the northern levies! Burn the frontier settlements! If we do not strike Dever now, they will think us weak and take the entire valley before winter!”

Darius stood up slowly from his seat at the center table. He did not shout; he simply stepped forward into the well of the council, his broad shoulders squared, his gaze cutting across the room with the force of an oncoming storm.

Silence spread outward from him like a ripple in water.

“You speak of war, Torval, as if it were a game of dice played in an alehouse,” Darius said, his voice deep, clear, and ringing with absolute authority. “You want to march forty thousand men into the northern passes when the grain harvest is barely half gathered. If you march now, you won't need the clans of Dever to kill your soldiers—the frost and famine will do it for them by the first snow.”

“The Speaker protects his clan friends!” Malakor sneered, leaning forward over the dais, his thin lips curling in disgust. “Darius Spencer has always urged appeasement! Perhaps the Speaker is more loyal to the merchants of Dever than to the King who raised him!”

The accusation hung in the air like a poisoned blade. Several guards shifted nervously, their hands resting on their hilts.

Darius didn't flinch. He walked directly to the foot of the Sun Throne, stopping ten paces from Malakor.

“I have bled for this crown on four battlefields, Inquisitor,” Darius said with deadly calm. “I held the Red Gap against twelve thousand raiders while you were safe in your cloister lighting candles and counting tithes. Do not speak to me of loyalty.”

Darius turned to face King Aldous, his tone softening with genuine sorrow. “Sire. I have examined your son’s body with Coroner Murat. I have walked the chamber where he fell. Valen was not killed by a Dever border clan. The Dever have neither the knowledge nor the means to craft weapons that leave metallic filaments in human bone. If you launch an army north today, you will be striking at an empty shadow—while the real assassin sits in your city, laughing behind your back.”

King Aldous shifted on his throne, his hands gripping the carved lion heads of the armrests until his skin went white. “Then who, Darius? Who killed my boy? If not the heretics... then who?”

Darius reached into his tunic and produced the metallic crow feather Arianna had recovered from the prince’s chambers. He held it aloft for the council to see. The silver-black metal caught the afternoon sun streaming through the stained glass, casting cold, razor-sharp reflections across the stone.

“A remnant of the First Men,” Darius announced, his voice dropping into grave certainty. “The prince was searching for something ancient buried beneath Oakhaven. Someone killed him to take it. Give me three days, Your Grace. Let me trace this steel to its forge. If I find the border clans are involved, I will lead the vanguard myself. But give me three days to find the truth.”

Malakor stepped forward, raising his staff. “The Church cannot abide delay while evil festers in the—”

“Enough!” King Aldous barked suddenly, his voice cracking with sovereign fury. The old king stood, swaying slightly on his feet. “The Speaker has his three days. Captain Vane, double the palace guard. No one leaves Oakhaven without my royal seal. And Inquisitor... keep your zealots away from the gates until Darius reports back.”

Aldous turned and retreated through the private curtain behind the throne, leaving the council in stunned, muttering disarray.

As the lords began to disperse, Darius caught Lady Arianna standing in the shadows of the gallery above. She met his gaze, her expression unreadable, before stepping back into the darkness of the archway.

## Chapter Five: The Prince's Secret

Midnight fell over Oakhaven like a shroud of black velvet.

A cold drizzle swept through the royal courtyards, muffling the tread of the night sentries pacing the ramparts. In the western tower of the palace, deep within the private wing of the royal library, Darius and Arianna worked behind locked doors.

Prince Valen’s private study had been searched by the Royal Guard that afternoon, but they had found only what an ordinary soldier expected to find: poetry books, training manuals, and love letters from court ladies.

Arianna knew better.

She knelt before the heavy oak fireplace, running her fingers along the soot-stained bricks of the hearth. “The prince was careless in many things, Darius, but not in his obsession. For two years, he visited the archive vault in the dead of night. He bribed lower librarians for scraps of maps dating back to the Age of Ruin.”

“Maps of what?” Darius asked, holding a lantern aloft as he examined the spines of the tomes on the shelves.

“The Under-Crypts,” Arianna replied. With a soft *click*, one of the hearthstones depressed an inch into the floor. A hidden seam in the oak paneling beside the chimney popped open, revealing a narrow vertical recess lined with lead sheeting.

Arianna reached inside and withdrew a thick leather-bound journal fastened with a brass clasp. The leather was worn, scarred with water damage, and embossed with a stylized crest: a skull entwined with silver circuitry.

Darius stepped over, resting the lantern on the writing desk as Arianna opened the book.

The pages were covered in Prince Valen’s cramped, manic handwriting, interspersed with meticulous hand-drawn sketches. There were anatomical drawings of human neural pathways overlapping schematic diagrams of ancient electronic circuits; chemical formulas for compounds that defied modern alchemical knowledge; and repeated transcripts of an ancient word written in archaic Latin script:

*MORTIS.*

“Mortis,” Darius murmured, reading the prince’s notes aloud. “*The final protocol. The automated awakening. When the flesh rots, the steel endures.* What did he think he was doing?”

“He thought he was seeking immortality,” Arianna said, her voice dropping into a reverent whisper as she turned the yellowed pages. “Like every foolish young prince who realizes that crowns rust and kings bleed. Valen discovered that Oakhaven was not founded by the first barbarian warlords who settled this valley. The city was built directly atop an ancient Precursor redoubt—a subterranean complex sealed during the final days of the First Age.”

She pointed to an intricate fold-out map drawn on parchment. It depicted the sewer network and ancient catacombs beneath Oakhaven, descending far below the city’s drainage sumps into a massive circular chamber marked in red ink:

*The Vault of the Crow.*

“According to Valen’s notes,” Arianna continued, her eyes reflecting the amber lantern light, “the vault contains the bio-synthetic core of an ancient defense intelligence. A machine that was engineered to synthesize vaccines, manipulate biological tissue, and control automated warrior constructs. But the machine required an organic host—a neural interface to bridge human consciousness with the machine grid.”

Darius looked up from the journal, a sickening realization washing over him. “Valen was trying to interface with it.”

“Not trying,” Arianna corrected, her voice chillingly calm. “He succeeded. He broke the outer seals three nights ago. He took an artifact from the vault—a cranial bio-matrix. But the interface rejected his neural profile. The machine’s security subroutines classified him as an unauthorized breach.”

“And it sent an extraction unit to reclaim the relic,” Darius said, looking at the metallic crow feather resting on the desk. “The shadow in his bedchamber wasn't an assassin sent by Dever. It was the vault's automated security.”

“Precisely,” Arianna said. She closed the journal with a sharp snap and slid it into the inner folds of her velvet robe. “And if Inquisitor Malakor leads forty thousand men to war over an ancient security glitch, he will march this kingdom into annihilation.”

“Then we have to go down there,” Darius said, reaching for his sword belt. “We have to seal that vault before someone else triggers the defense grid.”

Arianna looked at him, her dark eyes glittering in the gloom. “Yes, Darius. We must go down into the dark. But we will need a guide who knows the catacombs better than the living.”

“Murat,” Darius nodded. “He knows every tunnel beneath this city.”

“Then let us visit our undertaker,” Arianna whispered, extinguishing the lantern with a wave of her pale hand, plunging the room into shadow. “Before the hunters find him first.”

## Chapter Six: Shadows in the Catacombs

The stench of rotting sewage and stagnant water was suffocating.

Darius, Arianna, and Coroner Murat waded through knee-deep mire along the vaulted brick tunnels of Oakhaven’s primary sewer trunk. Murat led the way, holding an iron pole topped with an oil lamp that cast long, jittery shadows against the slime-slicked walls. In his left hand he carried a heavy cleaver he normally used for amputations; Darius walked close behind him with his broadsword drawn, his senses stretched to the breaking point.

“These tunnels date back four hundred years,” Murat grunted over his shoulder, stepping carefully over a pile of sodden timber. “Beyond the third sluice gate, the brick ends and the old Roman masonry begins. Beyond that... is stone no mason in our history ever cut.”

“How much further to the lower breach?” Darius asked, wiping sewer condensation from his forehead.

“Half a league,” Murat replied. “Directly beneath the Cathedral of Aurtus. The priests think they're praying over holy ground, but the foundations sit on hollow bedrock.”

As they rounded a wide bend where four tributary channels converged, Murat abruptly halted, raising his hand.

The water had stopped flowing.

In the basin ahead, three bodies lay half-submerged in the muck. They were dressed in the rough homespun tunics of city canal workers, but their bodies were contorted in unnatural, agonizing angles.

Darius stepped forward, his sword leveled. “Murat, illuminate them.”

The mortician raised the lamp.

The canal workers’ faces were gone—burned away by the same crystalline black necrosis that had consumed Prince Valen. From their open mouths sprouted thick clusters of translucent blue fungal filaments that pulsed with faint, rhythmic light, feeding on the decaying tissue.

“Merciful gods,” Murat whispered, crossing himself with his bloody cleaver. “The contagion... it’s spreading from the vault.”

A sudden, sharp metallic clicking sounded from the ceiling above.

Darius looked up.

Clinging to the curved brick ceiling thirty feet above were three figures. They were not human—not anymore. They possessed the emaciated, elongated frames of men, but their skin was gray and leathery, fused with strips of blackened carbon-fiber plating. Their fingers had been replaced by razor-sharp surgical blades, and their eyes burned with twin pinpricks of crimson light.

“Ambush!” Darius roared, lunging forward as the first creature dropped from the ceiling with a deafening screech.

The beast struck the water like an artillery shell, sending a wave of foul sludge crashing over Darius’s chest. A three-pronged steel blade lashed toward his throat.

Darius ducked under the whistling arc, stepping into the creature’s guard. With a veteran’s brutal efficiency, he brought the pommel of his broadsword down across the side of its skull, shattering bone and glass lenses, then pivoted and drove his steel blade clean through its chest.

The creature didn't scream. It hissed like boiling oil, and instead of blood, a torrent of pressurized black hydraulic fluid sprayed over Darius’s cuirass, hissing as it ate into the leather.

To his right, Murat swung his heavy cleaver with desperate, frantic strength, burying the blade into the shoulder of the second creature as it lunged for his throat. The beast barely flinched, backhanding the mortician with a force that sent Murat flying into the brick wall, his lamp shattering into the water.

Darkness crashed over the tunnel, illuminated only by the faint blue glow of the fungi and the red eyes of the third construct.

The third beast bounded past Darius, its blades clicking in an executioner’s tempo, heading straight for Lady Arianna, who stood motionless in the middle of the channel.

“Arianna, move!” Darius yelled, tearing his sword free from the first creature’s carcass and scrambling through the water.

Arianna did not run.

She did not draw a weapon.

As the steel-bladed monstrosity lunged for her throat, Arianna simply raised her right hand and spoke a single, guttural word in a language that felt like jagged glass scraping across Darius’s mind:

“*C E A S E.*”

A blinding pulse of obsidian-black energy erupted from Arianna’s palm.

The shockwave blasted the water outward in a fifty-foot circle, slamming Darius against the conduit wall. The attacking creature froze mid-air, suspended in the space between heartbeats. The metallic filaments across its limbs flared brilliant violet, then violently reversed course, turning inward and crushing its own mechanical chassis until its spine snapped with the sound of a falling tree.

The broken machine splashed lifelessly into the water.

Darius pushed himself up from the sludge, coughing and gasping for air, staring in absolute disbelief at the woman before him.

Arianna stood untouched by water or grime. Around her fingertips, tiny arcs of violet electrical current danced like living serpents, slowly absorbing into her pale skin. Her eyes, normally dark and calculating, were completely black—the whites swallowed by deep, bottomless void.

“Arianna...” Darius whispered, his sword trembling in his grip. “What did you just do?”

Arianna slowly turned her head toward him. The void receded from her eyes, returning to their familiar, calm gray, though her voice carried an unnatural resonance that vibrated through the stones.

“I reclaimed what was mine, Lord Speaker,” she said softly. “Now come. The way to the vault is clear. And our King has very little time left to live.”

## Chapter Seven: The Gathering Storm

When Darius and Arianna emerged from the secret crypt exit into the palace gardens, the morning sky over Oakhaven was the color of bruised iron.

The city was in chaos.

From the high battlements, thick columns of black smoke rose into the overcast sky from the merchant quarters and the lower docks. The bronze bells of the Cathedral of Aurtus were tolling in wild, erratic rhythms—the death knell of the kingdom.

Captain Vane met them at the garden gate, his tabard covered in soot and his face lined with exhaustion. A squad of twenty Royal Guardsmen stood behind him with crossbows loaded and halberds lowered.

“Speaker!” Vane gasped, rushing forward to grip Darius’s arm. “Thank God you're alive. We thought you were dead in the warrens.”

“What has happened?” Darius demanded, wiping dried sewer muck from his face.

“The King has collapsed,” Vane said, his voice cracking with panic. “At midnight, His Grace began coughing black blood. By dawn, the same blackened runes that killed the Prince began spreading across his throat. The physicians can do nothing. He is delirious, calling for his son.”

“And Malakor?” Arianna asked, stepping forward, her voice chillingly composed.

Vane swallowed hard, glancing at the distant cathedral towers. “The High Inquisitor declared that the King has been cursed by Dever blood-witches. He mobilized the Temple Guard two hours ago. They’ve sealed the city gates. They are rounding up every foreigner, scholar, and alchemist in the lower city and marching them to the execution pyres in Market Square. He has declared himself Lord Regent until an Inquisition trial can be held.”

“A religious coup,” Darius growled, his knuckles whitening on his sword hilt. “He’s using the King’s illness to purge the council and seize the crown.”

“There’s worse, my lord,” Vane continued, leaning close so the guards couldn't hear. “Malakor has dispatched a contingent of thirty templars to the library. They’re burning the historical archives. He says the ancient books are the source of the curse.”

Darius turned to Arianna, his eyes narrowing in suspicion. “He's burning the evidence. He doesn't want anyone to discover what Prince Valen found beneath the cathedral.”

“He is a frightened dog barking at thunder,” Arianna replied, her dark eyes staring toward the Great Keep where the royal banner fluttered at half-mast. “Malakor believes he can burn the past. But the past has already awakened.”

She turned to Darius, her gaze pinning him with an intensity that sent a shiver down his spine. “The King will not survive the night, Darius. If Malakor takes the throne room, this entire realm will drown in fire. You are the Lord Speaker. By the laws of the First Dynasty, the Speaker holds sovereign authority to convene the High Court and challenge the Regent’s mandate.”

“A challenge means civil war,” Darius said. “My personal garrison is only five hundred men. The Temple Guard has three thousand in the city alone.”

“You will not fight them with swords, Darius,” Arianna said, a strange, terrible smile playing at the corners of her lips. “You will fight them with the truth. Call the banners. Summon every loyal lord to the Great Hall tonight for the emergency conclave. Let Malakor bring his templars. Let him bring his holy staff.”

“And you?” Darius asked, watching her carefully. “What will you do?”

Arianna smoothed the velvet of her dark robes, looking toward the royal crypts where the ancient machines slept in the deep stone.

“I am going to prepare the coronation,” she said softly.

Before Darius could question her, Arianna turned and swept toward the private entrance of the keep, her dark cloak billowing in the cold wind like the wings of a descending crow.

Darius watched her go, a knot of dread tightening in his chest. In decades of war and statecraft, he had faced warlords, kings, and assassins.

Yet as he looked upon the woman he had trusted for thirty years, Darius Spencer knew with cold, absolute certainty:

## Chapter Eight: The Searing Brand

“Sire,” Darius whispered, dropping to one knee beside the bed.

## Chapter Nine: The Burning Library

*P R O J E C T   M O R T I S*
*RESTORATION FACILITY SEVEN*

## Chapter Eleven: The Cradle of the First Age

“Lord Speaker!” Torval shouted, stepping forward. “We thought you were arrested by the Inquisitor!”

“Malakor is a traitor to the realm,” Darius announced, his voice carrying to every corner of the vaulted hall. “He breached the royal crypts, desecrated the King’s dead son, and murdered royal officers in the dark. King Aldous did not name him Regent. He gave this seal to me.”

Darius held aloft his hand. The golden dragon devouring its tail caught the torchlight, undeniable and absolute.

A murmur of shock rippled through the gathered lords.

“The King is gone,” Darius continued, his steel-gray eyes locking with each warlord in turn. “His son is gone. But Eldoria is not dead. We do not kneel to religious tyrants who burn books and murder children in the streets. I have convened the Emergency Conclave under the First Charter. Tonight, we purge the city of the Inquisition.”

“And who will sit upon the Sun Throne?” Torval challenged, his hand resting on his axe. “You, Darius? Will the Speaker crown himself King?”

Darius paused.

From the open rafters above, a single black feather floated down through the smoky air. It spun in lazy circles, catching the firelight, before embedding itself point-first into the center of the oak council table with a sharp, metallic *thunk*.

The feather was razor-sharp, flexible steel, gleaming with iridescent violet luminescence.

The lords shrank back from the table in startled silence.

“The throne,” Darius said, his voice dropping into a register of icy finality, “has already chosen its master.”

## Chapter Seventeen: Whispers in the Rookery

In the highest room of the western tower—the ancient royal rookery—the cold was absolute.

The floor was littered with shattered pigeon cages, crushed glass, and severed iron chains. Hundreds of metallic constructs—the mechanical crows of Project Mortis—roosted along the crossbeams of the timber ceiling, their crimson eyes blinking in hypnotic synchronization.

In the center of the chamber stood Arianna.

She was draped in robes woven from living shadow and flexible metallic scales that rippled like oil on water. Her long black hair floated gently around her shoulders as if defying gravity, and across her pale brow rested a slender circlet of beaten black iron set with a single pulsing violet crystal.

Darius stepped into the rookery, his broadsword sheathed, his breath pluming in the freezing air.

“They are terrified of you, Arianna,” he said quietly, stopping ten paces away.

She turned slowly, her movements fluid and impossibly graceful. The swirling obsidian void within her eyes had settled into an eerie, glowing twilight—deep black pupil surrounded by an iris of liquid violet light.

“Fear is an effective catalyst, Darius,” she said, her voice harmonizing with the distant hum of the underground reactors. “It strips away pride, greed, and vanity. In their fear, they will obey.”

“They are men, not machines,” Darius countered, taking a step forward. “You cannot rule human beings through cold algorithms and terror. The First Age tried that. Their cities are ruins; their people are dust.”

“The First Age failed because their leaders were divided,” Arianna said, lifting her pale hand. A construct dove from the rafters, landing softly upon her forearm, its wing-blades settling with a gentle metallic hiss. “One faction built weapons; another built laws; a third built churches. They tore themselves apart from within. Mortis was designed to unify the shattered pieces under a single, unyielding will.”

She looked at him with an expression that held a flicker of ancient, mournful affection.

“Do you know why I chose you, Darius?”

“You didn't choose me,” Darius said. “I was the Speaker who answered the King's summons.”

“I chose you thirty years ago,” she said softly, stepping closer. “When you rode back from the Red Gap with four arrow wounds in your shoulder, carrying the body of a peasant boy who had died holding your banner. You were the only man in this corrupt court who understood the value of sacrifice. Who understood that peace is not bought with gold, but paid for in blood.”

She reached out, her cool, smooth fingertips lightly touching his jawline. Darius felt the hair on his arms stand up as a faint static charge tingled against his skin.

“Tonight, Malakor will launch his assault on the keep,” she whispered. “He will bring his remaining fanatics to burn the hall. When he does... let him come.”

“And you?”

“I will deliver justice,” she said. “The kind of justice men have forgotten how to give.”

## Chapter Eighteen: The Night of Cleansing

At the second hour of night, the gates of the High Keep were breached.

Four hundred Inquisitorial templars, whipped into a religious frenzy by Malakor’s sermon, smashed through the outer barricades with a heavy iron-headed battering ram. Flaming arrows arced through the black sky like falling stars, igniting the stables and storehouses in the outer bailey.

“Hold the stairs!” Captain Vane roared, his voice hoarse over the din of battle.

Royal Guardsmen formed a shield wall at the top of the marble grand staircase leading into the keep. Crossbow bolts rattled off heavy steel breastplates; blades clanged against bronze rims; and the stench of blood and burning oil filled the courtyard.

Darius fought at the center of the line, his broadsword flashing in the torchlight.

A templar charged him with a heavy halberd; Darius stepped inside the sweeping strike, catching the haft on his forearm guard, and slashed upward, his blade severing the leather ties of the man’s helm. He followed through with a crushing kick that sent the templar tumbling down the stairs, sweeping three others off their feet.

Despite their discipline, the Royal Guard was being steadily pushed back. For every templar who fell, two more surged through the breach, their eyes wild with fanatic devotion.

“They have the ram!” Vane yelled, parrying a spear thrust. “If they breach the inner doors, the lords are finished!”

At the base of the stairs, Brother Corvus—bandaged, one eye blinded, and swinging his heavy spiked mace with single-minded fury—shattered the center of the defensive line. Two royal guardsmen were thrown aside, their breastplates dented inward.

“Where is your Speaker now?” Corvus bellowed, spraying bloody spit through his broken teeth. “Where is your witch?”

Darius stepped into the breach, raising his broadsword with two hands. “Right here, Corvus.”

Corvus roared and swung his mace in a horizontal arc designed to take Darius’s head from his shoulders.

Darius ducked beneath the whistling steel, feeling the wind of the spikes brush his cheek. He stepped forward, lunging with all his weight, driving his steel blade through the chink beneath Corvus’s armpit.

Steel struck bone; Corvus gasped, his mace clattering to the flagstones. The brute staggered, looking down in disbelief at the sword buried three feet into his chest.

Darius ripped the blade free. Corvus collapsed onto his face in the mud, dead before his body stopped twitching.

The templars hesitated, their momentum broken.

Then, from the dark sky above, came a sound like the tearing of silk multiplied by ten thousand.

The storm had arrived.

## Chapter Nineteen: The Wings of Iron

The sky above Oakhaven erupted in a vortex of black wings.

It was not dozens of constructs, nor hundreds. It was thousands.

From the subterranean shafts of the city, from the ancient drainage tunnels, and from the hollow mountain beneath the Great Keep, the entire mechanized aerial legion of Project Mortis darkened the night.

The swarm did not screech; it descended in terrifying, disciplined silence, broken only by the rhythmic whisper of steel feathers cutting through the rain.

The first wave struck the templar archers on the ramparts.

With pinpoint, mechanical precision, the constructs dove from the clouds, their razor-sharp wing-blades severing bowstrings, eyes, and jugular veins before the men could even cry out. Crossbows clattered to the stones; flaming pitch pots overturned, spilling down the battlements into the enemy lines below.

“Archers, shoot them down!” Malakor’s captains screamed from the courtyard. “Shoot the beasts!”

Crossbow bolts were loosed into the darkness; the steel darts sparked harmlessly off the flexible titanium scales of the flock.

The second wave fell upon the main body of the Inquisition in the courtyard.

It was not a battle; it was an automated execution. Constructs swept through the ranks in interlocking spiraling grids, their wing-edges shearing through mail, plate, and flesh. Men dropped their shields and ran for the gates, but the gates were already sealed by iron constructs perched along the portcullis.

In the center of the courtyard, surrounded by his personal guard of twenty elite knights, High Inquisitor Malakor waved his burning staff in frantic, blind arcs.

“Back! In the name of Aurtus, I command you back!” he shrieked.

A single, colossal construct—six feet in wingspan, its plumage gleaming with obsidian iridescence—swooped low through the courtyard. Its talons caught the burning staff, wrenching the heavy silver relic from Malakor’s grip and snapping the seasoned ash shaft in two like a dry twig.

The flock parted like a curtain of living black velvet.

Walking through the carnage of the courtyard, untouched by blood or flame, was Lady Arianna.

She moved with absolute, terrifying calm. Around her shoulders, thousands of steel feathers ruffled in the wind. Behind her, floating silently above the flagstones, were four towering combat constructs—eight-foot-tall mechanized humanoids forged from the same gray Precursor alloy, their arms ending in heavy rotary blades and plasma cutting torches.

The surviving templars threw their swords into the dirt and dropped to their knees, weeping and pleading for mercy.

Arianna did not look at them.

She looked toward the open double doors of the Great Hall, where the lords of the realm stood frozen in absolute, paralyzed terror.

“Darius,” she commanded, her double-toned voice echoing across the courtyard with the authority of an empress. “Bring the lords to the throne room.”

## Chapter Twenty: The Fractured Realm

The Grand Throne Room was bathed in the harsh, flickering glare of chemical flares.

All fifty lords of the realm, their faces ashen, their hands trembling, knelt along the marble floor before the Sun Throne. Flanking the walls of the hall were twenty mechanical humanoid constructs of the First Men, standing motionless as iron statues, their optical lenses glowing a soft, menacing violet.

High Inquisitor Malakor knelt at the foot of the dais, stripped of his ceremonial robes, his hands bound behind his back with heavy iron chains. Blood dripped from his broken nose onto the white marble.

Darius Spencer stood beside the empty Sun Throne, his bloody sword sheathed at his side, the royal signet ring visible on his hand.

The heavy bronze doors at the end of the hall opened.

Lady Arianna walked into the room.

The hall went dead silent, save for the rhythmic, whispered rustle of her metallic cloak. As she ascended the steps of the dais, the lords bowed their heads until their foreheads pressed against the cold stone, too terrified to meet her eyes.

She stopped before the Sun Throne.

She turned to face the assembly, her gaze sweeping over the kneeling nobility like a winter frost falling over dead grass.

“You are men of titles,” she said, her voice soft, melodic, yet vibrating through the stone with the force of an avalanche. “You call yourselves dukes, counts, and lords. You swore oaths to protect the weak, yet you allowed this zealot to burn your libraries and murder your citizens in the street.”

She looked down at Malakor.

“What was your holy crusade, Inquisitor?” she asked. “You claimed to serve God. But you only served your own fragile, terrified vanity.”

“Demon...” Malakor spat, coughing blood. “You are the abomination that destroyed the First Age. The Church... will never kneel to you. The faithful will rise...”

“There is no Church,” Arianna said simply.

She raised her right hand.

A slender, needle-like metallic filament shot from her index fingertip, penetrating Malakor’s skull through the center of his forehead with a sharp, sickening *snap*.

The Inquisitor’s body stiffened; his eyes rolled back, flaring bright violet for a single heartbeat, before his nervous system collapsed. His body slumped forward onto the marble floor, motionless and cold.

Arianna retracted the filament with a flick of her wrist.

She turned to Duke Torval, who knelt trembling at the front of the dais. “Duke of the Iron Valleys. Stand.”

Torval swallowed hard, rising slowly to his feet, his knees knocking beneath his heavy mail.

“Do you recognize the authority of the Sun Throne?” she asked.

Torval looked at Malakor’s corpse, looked at the towering Precursor constructs lining the walls, and finally looked into the bottomless obsidian void of Arianna’s eyes.

He dropped back onto both knees, pulling his broadsword from its scabbard and laying it flat upon the marble steps.

“I recognize the Sovereign,” Torval choked out, his voice cracking with utter submission. “Long live the Queen.”

One by one, the other forty-nine lords pulled their blades from their scabbards and laid them upon the floor, their voices rising in a desperate, unified chorus:

“Long live the Queen! Long live the Queen!”

Darius stood beside the throne, his knuckles white against his sword hilt. He had wanted to stop the civil war. He had wanted to save the kingdom from religious fanaticism.

He had succeeded.

And in doing so, he had handed humanity’s future to an immortal god of steel and shadow.

## Chapter Twenty-One: The Conclave of Iron

The emergency conclave did not debate; it received decrees.

Within three days of the Inquisitor’s execution, Oakhaven was transformed.

The red banners of the Church of Aurtus were torn down and burned in Market Square. In their place hung the new standard of the realm: an obsidian crow with outspread wings, framed by nine silver circuit-stars upon a field of midnight black.

The city gates were reopened, but they were no longer manned by corrupt municipal guards taking bribes from grain cartels. At each checkpoint stood twin humanoid constructs of Project Mortis, their optical sensors scanning every traveler for contagion, contraband, and illegal weaponry.

Crime vanished from the streets overnight. Thieves, extortionists, and black-market alchemists were hunted down within hours by airborne mechanical swarms that never slept, never grew weary, and never accepted payoffs.

In the Great Library, the fire had been extinguished. Under Darius’s personal supervision, scholars were returned to their desks, cataloging the recovered fragments of the First Age under the watchful eyes of silent mechanical sentinels.

In the High Keep’s council chamber, Darius sat at the head of the oak table, reviewing military manifests with Captain Vane.

“The northern lords have fully mobilized their levies, Lord Speaker,” Vane reported, placing a map of the border provinces on the table. “Not to attack Dever, but to fortify the passes. Duke Torval has already sent three thousand tons of iron ore to the capital for automated fabrication.”

“And Dever?” Darius asked.

“Their ambassadors arrived at the southern gate two hours ago,” Vane replied, shaking his head in disbelief. “They brought tribute. Gold, grain, and an offer of an eternal treaty. They heard what happened to the Inquisition. They want peace, Darius. Desperately.”

The door to the chamber clicked open.

Lady Arianna walked in, flanked by two constructs whose polished armor gleamed like liquid mercury. She wore a simple, elegant gown of midnight silk, but across her shoulders rested the mantle of metallic feathers that had become the terrifying symbol of her reign.

“You have done well, Lord Speaker,” she said, taking a seat at the right hand of the table.

“The border is secure, Your Grace,” Darius said, keeping his tone carefully neutral. “The grain reserves are being distributed to the lower sectors. The plague has been contained by the vault’s synthesis antivirals.”

“And yet your heart is troubled, Darius,” she said softly, studying his weathered face with her twilight eyes.

“You killed forty men in the courtyard, Arianna,” Darius said, looking directly into her eyes. “You executed the High Inquisitor without a trial before the lords.”

“I removed an infection,” she countered, her voice calm and reasonable. “Had Malakor lived, he would have organized a holy insurrection from a prison cell. A thousand more would have died in the streets. A sovereign does not negotiate with disease, Darius. A sovereign eradicates it.”

Darius leaned back in his chair, exhaling a long, weary breath. “And what of the throne? The lords demand an official coronation. They need to see a crown on your head, or the peace will fracture the moment the winter sets in.”

A faint, chilling smile touched Arianna’s lips.

“Then let us give them their coronation, Lord Speaker. Tomorrow at dawn.”

## Chapter Twenty-Two: The Broken Altar

The Cathedral of Aurtus was stripped of its gilded lies.

The golden idols of the sun god were dismantled and hauled down the marble steps by work crews; the stained-glass windows depicting the mythical ascension of the First Kings were replaced with clean, clear quartz glass that let the cold morning light pour into the nave without distortion.

In the center of the sanctuary, where the high altar of sacrifice had stood for three centuries, a new dais of polished black basalt had been erected.

On the morning of the coronation, five thousand citizens, soldiers, and nobles packed into the cathedral, their breath hanging in the frosty air. The silence was absolute; no hymns were sung, no incense was burned.

Darius Spencer walked down the central aisle, carrying the ancient crown of the First Men on a cushion of crimson velvet. The crown was not gold; it was forged from celestial meteoric iron, adorned with seven uncut violet star-sapphires recovered from the deep vault.

He climbed the basalt steps.

Arianna knelt before him on the cold stone. She wore robes of woven black scales that absorbed the morning sunlight, her head bowed in solemn humility that belied the terrifying power pulsing beneath her flesh.

Darius lifted the crown.

He felt the cold weight of history in his hands. He knew what this moment meant. He was not merely crowning a queen; he was inaugurating a new age of the world.

He lowered the iron circlet onto her obsidian hair.

“By the mandate of the First Men,” Darius spoke, his deep voice carrying across the silent thousands, “and by the sovereign authority of the people of Eldoria, I crown you Arianna: Protector of the Realm, Keeper of the Vault, and Sovereign of the Sun Throne.”

Arianna stood up.

As she rose to her full height, the violet star-sapphires in the crown flared with brilliant, blinding light.

A deafening chorus of a thousand metallic wings erupted from the cathedral rafters as the mechanical flock took flight, circling the vaulted dome in an intricate, synchronized spiral.

Arianna turned to face her kingdom.

She raised her right hand, her twilight eyes sweeping over the kneeling crowd.

“You have known fear,” she proclaimed, her double-toned voice harmonizing with the stones of the cathedral. “You have known hunger, disease, and the petty cruelty of mortal kings who traded your lives for vanity. That era is finished. Under this crown, there are no borders, no corrupt priests, and no forgotten dead. I give you order. I give you strength. I give you the future.”

The assembly roared.

It began with the soldiers in the front rows, spreading backward through the nobility, until five thousand voices shook the foundation of the cathedral in a thunderous, unified cry:

“HAIL THE QUEEN! HAIL THE BLACK QUEEN!”

Darius stood behind her on the basalt dais, watching the sea of raised fists.

He looked down at his own hand, at the gold dragon signet ring that had belonged to King Aldous.

The old world was gone. A new reign had begun.

And sitting upon its throne was the Black Queen.

## Chapter Twenty-Three: The Descent of the Queen

The transformation of the realm was neither slow nor gentle.

Within six months of the coronation, the Black Queen’s mandate extended across the four kingdoms. The border fortresses of Dever surrendered their keys without a single arrow loosed; the mountain clans of the north pledged fealty in exchange for automated agricultural seed and geothermal heating grids; and the coastal merchant cartels were restructured into public distribution networks.

In Oakhaven, the old wooden tenements were cleared, replaced by clean, multi-tiered stone housing complexes heated by the subterranean generators of Project Mortis.

Yet the price of peace was paid in absolute obedience.

Darius walked through the royal gardens at twilight, finding Arianna standing atop the highest terrace overlooking the city. She was watching the construction of the Great Spire—a towering monolith of black titanium rising beside the keep, designed to house the master broadcast antenna of the Precursor grid.

“The lords of the eastern valleys are complaining, Arianna,” Darius said, leaning against the balustrade beside her.

“Let them complain,” she replied without turning. “Their grain yields have doubled, and their children no longer die of summer fever.”

“They complain because your constructs patrol their village squares,” Darius said gently. “They complain because every word spoken in the public taverns is recorded by the birds on the rooftops.”

Arianna turned to him, her twilight eyes reflecting the setting sun. “And do those taverns harbor rebellions, Darius? Do the lords plot civil war while their peasants starve?”

“No,” Darius conceded. “They do not.”

“Then my constructs have done their work,” she said softly. She reached out, her gloved fingers resting on the sleeve of his tunic. “You still look upon me with sadness, Darius. Why? We have built the paradise your King dreamed of.”

“A paradise where no one can speak freely is not a home, Arianna,” Darius said, meeting her gaze with an old soldier's unflinching honesty. “It is a very well-managed prison.”

Arianna was quiet for a long moment, watching the evening shadows lengthen across the valley.

“Perhaps,” she whispered, her voice carrying the cold weight of centuries yet to come. “But in a prison, the inmates survive the winter.”

## Chapter Twenty-Four: The Iron Coronation

One year after the fall of the Inquisition, the last resistance to the Black Queen’s rule was extinguished.

In the southern city of Corval, a cabal of exiled priests and disgruntled iron lords had gathered an army of eight thousand mercenaries, plotting to march on Oakhaven and restore the old feudal order.

They never crossed the river.

At midnight, before their vanguard could strike camp, the sky over their encampment was filled with ten thousand mechanical constructs. Chemical suppression gas was vented into their tents; automated heavy turrets deployed from the riverbeds; and by dawn, the mercenary army had surrendered their weapons without firing a single volley.

The leaders of the rebellion were brought to Oakhaven in chains.

They did not face an executioner’s block.

The Black Queen stood before them in the Grand Hall, surrounded by her council.

“You wished to return this realm to the age of kings,” she said to the kneeling rebel lords. “You wished to restore the nobility to their ancestral estates. You will have your wish.”

She signaled with her hand.

Two medical officers from the lower vault stepped forward, carrying silver hypodermic injectors containing the refined nanite matrix of Project Mortis.

“You will be returned to your estates,” the Queen decreed, her voice ringing cold and clear through the hall. “You will govern your people under the supervision of the local sentinels. But you will bear the seal of the crown within your blood. Should treason ever enter your hearts again, the machine will know. And your heart will cease to beat.”

One by one, the rebel lords were injected, their forearms flaring with the faint, glowing silver runes of the Mortis network before fading beneath the skin.

They were released, returning to their lands as governors of a sovereign who could hear their every thought.

Darius watched from his station at the Speaker’s desk.

He realized then the true genius—and the true horror—of the Black Queen. She did not rule through bloodbaths; she ruled through absolute, inescapable biological integration.

She was not merely their queen.

She was their nervous system.

## Chapter Twenty-Five: The First Pact

Late into the night, in the private solar of the High Keep where King Aldous had once wept for his son, Darius and Arianna sat across from one another at a small mahogany table.

A single bottle of twenty-year-old plum brandy sat between them, alongside two crystal glasses.

“I am stepping down, Arianna,” Darius said quietly, pushing his untouched glass aside.

Arianna looked up from a leather-bound administrative ledger, her violet eyes studying him with unreadable intensity. “You cannot step down, Darius. The people trust you. The lords fear me, but they revere the Lord Speaker. Without you between us, the friction will wear them away.”

“I am fifty-four years old,” Darius said, rubbing his tired face with both hands. “My hair is white. My sword arm aches when the rain falls. You have not aged a single day since you walked out of that sarcophagus. In ten years, I will be an old man dying in my bed. In thirty years, I will be dust. And you will still be sitting on that throne, looking as young and cold as the day we met.”

Arianna set down her quill. She stood up and walked around the table, stopping behind his chair.

She placed her hands gently upon his shoulders. Her touch was no longer freezing; the nanite matrix within her body had learned to mimic the warmth of human skin, comforting yet unnervingly steady.

“You will not die, Darius,” she whispered in his ear. “The vault has stasis cradles. It has genetic synthesis bays. I can give you the treatment. I can give you decades... centuries. You can stand beside me as the eternal Speaker of this realm.”

Darius closed his eyes, feeling the temptation pull at his weary soul like the undertow of a deep river.

To live forever. To see the world rebuilt. To watch humanity rise from the ashes of the First Age into the light of the stars.

Then he opened his eyes and looked at his reflection in the dark windowpane—an aging soldier with honor in his eyes and scars on his hands.

“No, Arianna,” Darius said softly. “Mortals must die. If we do not die, we forget what it means to bleed. We forget what it means to care for those who only have seventy years upon this earth.”

He reached up, placing his calloused hand over hers on his shoulder.

“I will serve you as Lord Speaker for as long as my breath lasts,” he promised. “I will be the voice of your people. I will pull your hand back when your cold machines wish to strike too hard. That is my pact with you. But when my time comes... you must let me sleep.”

Arianna stood silent behind him for a long, agonizing moment.

Then, with a gentle squeeze of his shoulders, she whispered:

“I accept your pact, Darius Spencer. Until the sleep comes.”

## Chapter Twenty-Six: The Legend of Mortis

By the thirtieth year after Prince Valen’s death, Eldoria had changed.

Under the reign of the Black Queen, the four kingdoms were forged into a single, unyielding empire. Roads of crushed granite and asphalt connected the distant mountain keeps to the coastal ports; aqueducts brought clean water to every desert outpost; and the famine that had haunted the centuries following the Fall was eradicated by automated hydroponic towers.

The stories of the bloody night in the cathedral faded into myth.

To the generation born under her banner, the Black Queen was not a monster from the ancient world; she was the eternal Mother of the Realm—a divine sovereign who watched over them from the black towers of Oakhaven, sending her mechanical crows to deliver medicine, carry messages, and strike down bandits before they could harm the innocent.

Yet in the shadows of the palace, the truth remained.

In the subterranean vaults beneath the cathedral, the machines of Project Mortis never stopped humming. Deep within the bedrock, automated fabrication bays forged legions of synthetic warriors, stockpiling weapons, ammunition, and energy cells for a war that had not yet arrived.

On the highest terrace of the Black Spire, the Queen stood watching the northern horizon.

She wore her armor of living black scales, the crown of seven violet star-sapphires gleaming upon her brow. Her hair moved with the wind, but her face remained as flawless and youthful as the day she had stepped into the sarcophagus three decades ago.

Beside her, leaning heavily on an iron cane, stood Lord Speaker Darius Spencer.

At eighty-two, Darius was an old lion whose roar had faded into a gravelly whisper. His shoulders were bent, his beard snow-white, but his eyes—clear, intelligent, and unyielding—still watched the world with a statesman’s sharp perception.

“The scouts report strange lights in the upper atmosphere, Arianna,” Darius said, his voice raspy as dry parchment. “Over the southern ice sheets. Relic energy signatures unlike anything our sensors have cataloged.”

“I felt them,” the Queen replied, her voice smooth and melodic, layered with the faint synthetic resonance of the machine core. “A stasis beacon has activated in the southern wastes. One of the ancient sleeper pods has initiated emergency re-entry.”

Darius looked at her, his brow furrowing with ancient curiosity. “A sleeper? From the First Age?”

“Yes,” she whispered, her twilight eyes turning toward the southern sky where the stars were beginning to emerge in the twilight. “A soldier of the Old World. His name is Marcus.”

Darius leaned against his cane, looking out over the peaceful, glowing city of Oakhaven.

“Will he bow to you?” Darius asked.

The Black Queen smiled—a cold, beautiful, and terrible expression that promised storms on the horizon.

“He will try to fight,” she said softly. “He will bring the weapons of the past to challenge the future. And like all mortal men who dare to dream of freedom... he will learn that the dark always claims its own.”

She turned to Darius, her hand gently resting on the old man’s arm, supporting his weight as the evening wind swept across the terrace.

“Come, Lord Speaker,” the Black Queen said. “Let us prepare the welcoming committee. The next age of mankind is about to begin.”
